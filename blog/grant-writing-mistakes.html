Grant Writing Mistakes That Cost Nonprofits Millions Every Year

Published on April 22, 2026 | By The Grant Finder

Grant writing is challenging work, but many nonprofit rejections result not from weak ideas or insufficient capacity, but from preventable mistakes. By understanding the most common errors that cause grant applications to be rejected, you can dramatically improve your funding success rate. Based on feedback from program officers and grant reviewers, here are the grant writing mistakes that cost nonprofits millions in unrealized funding every year.

1. Not Following the Funder's Guidelines Exactly

One of the most frustrating mistakes grant reviewers encounter is organizations that don't follow instructions. The funder specifies a 15-page limit, and you submit 18 pages. The funder requests Times New Roman 12-point font, and you submit Arial 11-point. The funder asks for a specific table of contents format, and you use a different structure.

While these may seem like minor issues, they signal disrespect for the funder's process and raise concerns about whether your organization will follow grant reporting requirements and other obligations carefully. Additionally, reviewers may use page limits and formatting specifications to manage their workload. An oversized submission may literally not be reviewed in its entirety because it's inconvenient to handle.

The solution is simple: create a checklist of every requirement in the funder's guidelines and verify that your application meets each requirement before submission. Have someone unfamiliar with your proposal review it against this checklist. This small investment of time can prevent automatic rejection.

2. Failing to Address the Funder's Priorities

Each funder has specific priorities that guide their grantmaking. A foundation focused on environmental sustainability won't fund job training programs, and a funder prioritizing youth education won't fund senior services. Yet many nonprofits submit generic proposals that could be submitted to any funder, regardless of their actual priorities.

Your proposal should explicitly connect your project to the funder's stated priorities. If the funder's guidelines state they prioritize "programs serving high-poverty neighborhoods," your proposal should specifically identify the neighborhoods you serve, provide demographic data, and explain why the funder's focus on this area aligns with your organization.

Even better, research the funder's previous grants. Review their grant-making history and see what types of organizations they've funded previously. Tailor your proposal to fit within this pattern. If they've primarily funded direct service organizations rather than advocacy groups, emphasize your direct service model. This targeted approach significantly improves your chances.

3. Vague and Unmeasurable Goals

Nonprofit leaders often struggle with writing measurable goals because measuring impact is genuinely difficult work. However, funders absolutely require specific, measurable objectives. Phrases like "improve student outcomes," "enhance community engagement," or "reduce homelessness" are too vague.

Instead, write objectives like "increase the graduation rate among program participants from 73 percent to 87 percent by June 2027" or "reduce average days to permanent housing for program participants from 120 days to 60 days." These objectives are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound.

For each objective, identify how you'll measure progress. Will you use standardized assessments, survey data, administrative records, or other methods? Specify the data source and collection timeline. When reviewers can understand exactly what success looks like and how you'll measure it, they're much more confident in your proposal.

4. Weak Problem Statements and Insufficient Needs Data

Your statement of need must establish that a genuine problem exists and is significant enough to warrant funding. Yet many proposals fail to adequately justify the need. Weak needs statements typically rely on anecdotal evidence or general statements without data backing.

Instead, ground your needs statement in hard data. Use census data, research studies, government reports, and service statistics to establish the scope and severity of the problem. If you're serving a specific neighborhood, provide demographic data and statistics about that neighborhood. If you're addressing a specific issue, cite research demonstrating the scale of the issue.

Additionally, provide evidence that the problem exists within your service area and affects the populations you serve. A national statistic about youth dropout rates isn't persuasive unless you can document that youth dropout rates are actually a problem in your community. This combination of national context and local specificity is most convincing.

5. Misaligned Budget and Narrative

Your budget and narrative must align perfectly. If your narrative describes hiring three full-time staff members, those positions must appear in your budget with appropriate salaries. If you describe purchasing specialized equipment, those purchases must be itemized in the budget. Discrepancies between what you describe and what you budget for create serious credibility problems.

Additionally, many nonprofits underestimate costs, creating unrealistic budgets. If you describe a comprehensive evaluation plan, your budget should include funds for an evaluator. If you describe providing intensive case management, your budget should reflect the staff time required. Conversely, if your budget includes significant funds that aren't explained in your narrative, reviewers wonder what those funds will actually support.

The solution is straightforward: write your narrative first, then develop your budget based on what you've described. After developing the budget, review it against your narrative to ensure every expense is explained.

6. Inadequate Evaluation Plans

Many nonprofits provide minimal evaluation plans, perhaps a single paragraph describing how they'll measure outcomes. Funders want to understand exactly how you'll measure success, when you'll collect data, who will be responsible for evaluation, and how you'll use evaluation findings.

A strong evaluation plan addresses these questions: What will you measure (process, output, and outcome measures)? How will you measure it (data sources and methods)? When will you collect data? Who will be responsible? How will you ensure data quality? How will you use findings to improve your program?

For many funders, particularly government funders, the evaluation plan is nearly as important as the project description. They want assurance that you'll honestly assess whether you're achieving your objectives and that you'll make adjustments if programs aren't working as planned.

7. Overlooking the Organizational Capacity Section

Many nonprofits treat the organizational capacity section as an afterthought, providing minimal information about their organization. Funders invest in organizations as much as projects, and they need to understand that your organization has the infrastructure and leadership to successfully implement the grant.

Your organizational capacity section should include information about your board (size, composition, relevant expertise), executive leadership (experience, relevant background), key program staff, relevant organizational history, similar projects you've successfully completed, and your infrastructure (financial management systems, evaluation capacity, technology systems).

If your organization is relatively new or this grant represents a significant expansion, proactively address this. Describe partnerships with experienced organizations, technical assistance you've secured, or staff you've hired to build capacity. Being honest about capacity building plans is more credible than pretending challenges don't exist.

8. Generic Letters of Support and Partner Commitment

Many nonprofits include generic letters of support from partners that add little value. These letters often don't reference the specific project, don't commit specific resources or roles, and don't demonstrate genuine partnership. Experienced reviewers can spot generic templates immediately.

Instead, request that partners provide specific letters that reference your project by name, describe their specific role in implementation, commit specific resources or contributions, and explain why they believe in your organization. A letter from a school principal saying "we support this youth program and will allow after-school activities to occur in our building" is much more valuable than "we support XYZ nonprofit's great work."

Additionally, include only letters from actual partners who will have meaningful roles in your project. One strong, specific letter is more valuable than five generic, superficial letters.

9. Missing or Weak Sustainability Plans

Many funders want to understand how you'll sustain successful programs after grant funding ends. Yet many nonprofits provide vague sustainability statements like "we'll secure additional funding" without explaining specifically how. This raises concerns about whether you've honestly assessed sustainability challenges.

A strong sustainability plan addresses what you'll do with the program after grant funding ends. Will you incorporate it into your annual budget? Will you seek permanent funding from another source? Will you phase it out responsibly? Will you transition it to a partner? Being specific about your sustainability strategy demonstrates sophisticated planning.

10. Rushing the Application Process

Finally, many nonprofit rejections result simply from rushing the application. Organizations that allow only one or two weeks to develop applications often produce proposals with typos, unclear writing, and incomplete sections. Organizations that submit applications the night of the deadline sometimes miss requirements or make careless errors.

Build adequate time into your grant development process. For most grants, allow at least four to six weeks for proposal development. This allows time for writing, review by board and staff, revision, and final proofreading. Many grants are lost not because the ideas aren't strong, but because the proposal was rushed and reflects that haste.

Conclusion

Grant rejections are disappointing, but most organizations will receive rejections regardless of their quality. However, many rejections can be prevented by avoiding these common mistakes. By following funders' guidelines precisely, tailoring your proposals to funders' priorities, providing specific measurable objectives, grounding your needs statement in data, aligning your budget and narrative, developing strong evaluation plans, demonstrating organizational capacity, securing meaningful partner letters, planning for sustainability, and allowing adequate time for proposal development, you'll dramatically improve your funding success rate. These mistakes are preventable—and avoiding them could mean the difference between rejection and funding for your nonprofit.
