How to Write a Grant Proposal That Actually Gets Funded

Published on March 18, 2026 | By The Grant Finder

Writing a winning grant proposal is both an art and a science. While there's no guaranteed formula for success, understanding what funders look for and structuring your proposal strategically can dramatically improve your chances of funding. This comprehensive guide will walk you through the essential elements of a grant proposal that gets results, incorporating insights from experienced grant reviewers and funding professionals.

The Executive Summary: Your Chance to Hook the Funder

The executive summary is arguably the most important section of your grant proposal. Many reviewers read only the executive summary before deciding whether to continue reading your full proposal. This is your opportunity to capture attention, articulate the problem you're addressing, and communicate why your organization is uniquely positioned to solve it.

Your executive summary should be concise, typically one to two pages, and should answer four critical questions: What is the problem? Who does it affect? Why does it matter? How will you solve it? Experienced grant writers know that executive summaries should be written last, after you've completed the rest of your proposal, because you'll have a complete picture of your project by then.

Avoid jargon, clichés, and vague language. Instead, use concrete numbers, compelling stories, and clear language. If you can reference statistics that demonstrate the problem's scale or severity, include them. Funders want to understand immediately why your project deserves their investment.

Statement of Need: Make the Case Compelling and Data-Driven

The statement of need is where you establish that a problem exists and that it's significant enough to warrant funding. This section requires both quantitative data and qualitative evidence. While numbers are important, equally important is making the funder feel the human impact of the problem.

Begin by establishing the problem with demographic data, research findings, and statistics from credible sources. Show the geographic scope of the problem and its local impact. Then, move from data to people by sharing stories, quotes from community members, or case studies that illustrate how the problem manifests in real lives.

Be specific about your target population. Rather than saying "low-income families need support," specify something like "single mothers with children under five in our service area earn an average of $18,000 annually, 67 percent below the federal poverty line." This specificity demonstrates that you've thoroughly assessed the need and understand your community.

Importantly, avoid making the need seem so overwhelming that it appears unsolvable. Instead, frame it as a significant challenge where your proposed intervention can make meaningful progress. Funders want to invest in solutions, not intractable problems.

Project Description: Show How You'll Solve the Problem

Your project description should provide a clear roadmap of how you'll address the need you've identified. Break your project into specific goals, objectives, and activities. Each goal should be broad and strategic, while objectives should be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART).

For each objective, describe the specific activities you'll implement and explain how these activities will help achieve your objective. This narrative should be logical and easy to follow. Many funders receive proposals where it's unclear exactly what the organization plans to do, so clarity is a significant advantage.

Include timelines showing when different project phases will occur. If your project involves multiple components or locations, make clear how these connect and complement each other. Additionally, explain how your project builds on previous successes or lessons learned from earlier pilot projects if applicable.

Goals and Objectives: Make Them SMART and Measurable

Funders invest in outcomes, not just activities. Your goals and objectives must be specific and measurable. Rather than writing "improve youth outcomes," specify "increase graduation rate among program participants from 73 percent to 87 percent by June 2027."

Each objective should include a measurable target, a timeline for achievement, and a specific responsible party. Funders appreciate when objectives are ambitious but realistic. If you typically serve 100 youth annually and are seeking funding to serve 105 youth, reviewers may question whether you've truly thought about program expansion.

Consider developing a logic model that shows the relationships between your activities, outputs, and outcomes. This visual representation helps reviewers quickly understand how your work will produce results. Many grant proposals benefit from including a one-page logic model as an appendix.

Evaluation Plan: Demonstrate Accountability and Learning

Your evaluation plan demonstrates that you'll measure whether your project achieved its objectives. This section should describe what you'll measure, how you'll measure it, and when you'll collect data. Specifically address process evaluation (did you implement the program as planned?) and outcome evaluation (did you achieve your objectives?).

Describe your data collection methods. Will you use surveys, interviews, focus groups, administrative data, or standardized assessment tools? Be specific about instruments you'll use and justify your choices. If you're adapting instruments from existing research, cite your sources.

Address how you'll use evaluation data. Will you provide quarterly reports to the funder? Will you modify the program based on early findings? Demonstrating that you'll use data for continuous improvement shows sophistication and commitment to effectiveness.

Budget and Budget Narrative: Make Numbers Tell Your Story

Your budget must align exactly with your project description. If you described hiring three new staff members, your budget should reflect their salaries. If you mentioned office rent, it should appear in the budget. Inconsistencies between narrative and budget raise red flags for reviewers.

Your budget narrative should explain significant line items, particularly personnel costs. For each staff position, briefly describe their role in the project. For equipment or technology, explain why it's necessary and how it supports your project. For indirect costs, clearly explain how they're calculated and what they support.

Demonstrate cost-effectiveness. Show that you've carefully thought through expenses and haven't included unnecessary or excessive costs. If salaries seem particularly high, explain why (perhaps this position requires specialized credentials). If you're leveraging significant in-kind contributions or partner resources, highlight these as evidence of community investment in your project.

Organizational Capacity: Build Confidence in Your Execution

Funders invest in organizations as much as projects. Your organizational capacity section should demonstrate that your organization has the infrastructure, experience, and staffing to successfully implement the proposed project. Include information about your board of directors, key staff members, organizational history, and relevant previous projects.

If you're new to grant implementation or this represents a significant expansion of your services, proactively address this. Describe partnerships with more experienced organizations, technical assistance you've secured, or additional staffing you've hired to build capacity. Being transparent about capacity gaps and your plan to address them is more effective than hoping reviewers won't notice.

Conclusion and Sustainability Plan: Think Beyond the Grant

Your conclusion should restate your project's significance and remind reviewers why their funding matters. Beyond the grant period, address how you'll sustain the project. Will you incorporate successful programs into your regular budget? Will you seek additional funding? Will you phase out the project responsibly?

Sustainability demonstrates that you see the funder's grant as an investment that will generate lasting benefit, not just short-term activity. This perspective aligns with how sophisticated funders think about their resources.

Final Editing and Review Tips

Before submitting, have multiple people review your proposal. Include board members, staff, and if possible, external reviewers unfamiliar with your organization. Fresh eyes catch inconsistencies and unclear passages that you've read over so many times they've become invisible.

Check that the proposal specifically addresses the funder's funding guidelines and priorities. If the funder values innovation, emphasize how your approach is innovative. If they prioritize serving underserved populations, highlight your organization's commitment to equity.

Follow all formatting requirements exactly. Use the requested font, page limits, and spacing. This attention to detail signals respect for the funder and demonstrates your organization's professionalism.

Conclusion

Writing a compelling grant proposal requires strategic thinking, clear writing, and meticulous attention to detail. By following the structure outlined in this guide and incorporating the insights from experienced grant reviewers, you'll create proposals that capture funders' attention and clearly communicate why your project deserves funding. Remember that grant writing is a skill that improves with practice. Each proposal you write teaches you something that will improve your future submissions. Start with these principles, remain flexible as you learn what works with different funders, and persist through rejections as you refine your approach.
